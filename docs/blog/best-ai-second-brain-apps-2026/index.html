Best AI Second Brain Apps in 2026, Honestly Ranked

The best AI second brain apps 2026 has to offer, ranked: Everpaper, Mem, ScreenPipe, Otter.ai, and Granola, with honest pricing, limits, and privacy tradeoffs.

By Marco Iannello · Updated August 4, 2026

A second brain is only as good as what it remembers. Most apps in this category remember what you bothered to type into them. One on this list remembers what you actually did all day, and that difference changes everything downstream: what the AI can answer, what it can connect, and whether you trust what it tells you.

We ranked the five best AI second brain apps in 2026 by capture effort and answer honesty, with data location as a hard filter. Everpaper is our product, so read the #1 spot with that in mind; we've tried to be as hard on ourselves as on everyone else.

How we evaluated

Four criteria, in the order we think they matter:

  1. Capture model. Does the brain fill itself, or do you feed it? A second brain that depends on your discipline degrades the week you get busy.
  2. Answer honesty. When the AI answers a question about your work, can you check it? Citations back to a real moment beat fluent confidence.
  3. Data location. On-device, encrypted cloud, or plain cloud the vendor can read? This decides who else gets to use your second brain.
  4. Scope. Whole working day, or meetings only? Meeting tools are real second brains for conversations and nothing else, and we rank them with that scope stated plainly.

1. Everpaper — the second brain that builds itself

Full disclosure: Everpaper is our product.

Overview. Everpaper is a private, on-device daybook for macOS. One capture stream feeds its Day Journal and meeting recorder, The Minutes. Second Brain then builds a cross-day knowledge graph from that record without you writing a note. Everpaper takes periodic screenshots of your front window (default every 2 minutes), runs OCR on-device, and records and transcribes meetings on the Neural Engine. The Second Brain section opens from the Index (⌘K) with tabs for Chat, Insights, Network, and Export (⌘1–⌘4).

What we like. The self-model is the point. From the captured days, Everpaper forms one-line beliefs about your work in plain language: people you keep dealing with, projects that recur, files you return to, and sites that matter. Each carries an honest confidence marker: (tentative) or (likely). A belief needs to appear across at least two days before it forms, and it decays after quiet days. Files or tickets you touched and then dropped get flagged as open loops, phrased as possibilities, not facts. All of that is computed locally and readable with no AI provider connected.

Connect a provider (Apple Foundation Models or Ollama keep it fully on your Mac) and you get the Reflection: two or three grounded observations about how you actually work, each pointing back to captured evidence. Ask the Second Brain a question and every answer is verified against captured history before it's shown: unsupported claims get stripped, and relational answers retain [#n] citations to evidence in the captured record.

Drawbacks. macOS 14.2+ only, Apple Silicon only, so Intel Macs are out, and there's no iOS, Windows, Linux, Android, or web version. Access is limited during the private beta. The Second Brain has a readiness gate: it stays in "Still learning you" until you've captured 5 days, 300 frames, 60 distinct things, and 3 recurring threads. And the AI features (Reflection, chat, Ask the Book) wait until you configure a provider; capture, OCR, search, beliefs, and the graph never need one, but the generative layer does.

Pricing. In private beta with limited spots, with a founding-license option for early supporters, not a monthly subscription. No public price as of 2026-08-04.

Best for. Mac-based knowledge workers who want a second brain that fills itself, stays on their machine, and shows its work. If the rest of this list feels like homework, this is the one built for you. See the Second Brain docs and how recall works.

2. Mem — the best manual-capture brain, with a cloud catch

Overview. Mem is an AI-native note app: you capture notes manually, and its Mem Agent chats across everything you've saved, auto-organizes, and surfaces related notes. The current product line includes a $99/month Proactive tier whose agent tracks decisions and open loops and briefs you before meetings.

What we like. Zero-friction capture plus genuinely strong semantic search. Reviewers consistently describe the workflow as "toss it in Mem and find it later," and Mem Agent is about as good as AI chat over a personal knowledge base gets. Pricing is simple and honest: flat $12/mo Pro, no per-seat math. Export is one click to Markdown, and Mem commits in writing to never selling your data or mining it for ads.

Drawbacks. Everything hinges on you typing things in. Skip a week and your second brain has a hole shaped exactly like that week. The deeper issue is architectural: Mem's own pricing FAQ states your data "is encrypted at rest with AES-256 and in transit with TLS, but it is not e2e encrypted... because our core focus is to provide you with intelligence on top of your Mem." Mem's servers can read your notes; it is cloud-only with no local-vault mode, and sign-in is Google-only. The same official pricing page describes Agent chat across saved material but does not document clickable note or PDF citations. Users on r/PKMS also report stalled development and a "self-organizing" promise that underdelivers in practice. Free tier is effectively a trial at 25 notes/month.

Pricing (checked 2026-08-04, get.mem.ai/pricing). Free $0 (25 notes/mo, 25 Mem Agent messages, 25 PDF pages/mo). Mem Pro $12/mo, monthly billing only (unlimited notes, 250 Agent messages, unlimited PDF chat, connected email, API). Mem Proactive $99/mo after a 7-day trial (proactive briefings, open-loop tracking, check-ins).

Best for. Disciplined note-takers who want strong AI chat over what they save and don't mind cloud processing. Mem rewards a consistent capture habit; it doesn't replace one.

3. ScreenPipe — the developer's local recorder

Overview. ScreenPipe is a source-available, local-first recorder for macOS, Windows, and Linux: 24/7 screen and mic capture at a default 1 FPS, on-device OCR, local Whisper transcription, and a search API at localhost:3030. On top sit "pipes" (scheduled AI agents defined as a pipe.md prompt plus a schedule) and an MCP server that plugs your captured history into Claude, Codex, Cursor, or ChatGPT.

What we like. Genuinely local-first with a real offline mode: capture, OCR, local Whisper, search, and the API all work with no network. PII redaction is a default-on flag. It's the most developer-friendly thing on this list: local SQLite, an HTTP API, MCP integration, and a pipes framework that can run on local models via Ollama. And because it records system audio and screen rather than joining calls as a bot, it works with any meeting software.

Drawbacks. It's infrastructure, not a second brain. There's no built-in knowledge graph, no beliefs, no self-model. You get raw capture and an API, and assembling understanding on top is your project. An Ask HN thread asks whether anyone understands the pricing. In June 2026 the source moved from MIT to a commercial license for business use, which stung parts of the community. ScreenPipe's official FAQ does not document speaker identification or diarization, and the Linux desktop app is partial.

Pricing (checked 2026-08-04, supplied ScreenPipe pricing page). Free is $0 with a 7-day Business trial, local capture, AI search, meeting notes, 50+ integrations, and 10 AI credits/month. Basic is $25/month or $250/year, with 150 credits and unlimited user-installed scheduled tasks and history. Business is $50/seat/month or $500/seat/year, with 400 credits, frontier Claude and GPT models, cloud sync, automations, and team workspace.

Best for. Developers who want a local memory substrate to build on, and who'd rather wire pipes and MCP themselves than trust anyone's opinionated graph.

4. Otter.ai — a meeting-only second brain

Overview. Otter is the veteran cloud meeting bot: OtterPilot joins your Zoom, Google Meet, and Teams calls, transcribes live, identifies speakers, captures slides, and gives you AI chat within and across your whole meeting archive. As a second brain, its scope is exactly one thing, what was said in your meetings, and within that scope it's deep.

What we like. Mature live transcription with real-time editing, highlights, and comments. The cross-meeting AI chat and search is a genuine meeting memory: "what did we decide about the pricing change" answered across months of calls. Platform coverage is the broadest here (bot plus desktop, iOS, Android, Chrome extension), and the business integrations (Salesforce, HubSpot, Zapier, API on Enterprise) are uncommon among notetakers.

Drawbacks. It remembers meetings and nothing else: no documents, no browsing, no work between calls. Everything is cloud: audio and transcripts upload to AWS in the US, and Otter's June 2026 privacy policy reserves the right to train "proprietary AI technology on de-identified audio recordings and on transcriptions." No offline mode exists. The consent question is real. A consolidated federal class action (In re Otter.AI Privacy Litigation, N.D. Cal.) alleges recording without all-party consent, with a motion-to-dismiss hearing held in May 2026. Accuracy complaints ("hit or miss," speaker misattribution) persist in reviews, and the minute caps on lower tiers force usage tracking.

Pricing (checked 2026-08-04, otter.ai/pricing). Basic free (300 min/mo, 30 min per conversation, 3 lifetime file imports). Pro $16.99/user/mo monthly or $8.49/user/mo annual (1,200 min/mo, 90 min/meeting). Business $30/user/mo monthly or $24/user/mo annual (4 hrs/meeting, admin features). Enterprise custom.

Best for. Teams whose institutional memory genuinely lives in meetings, want a searchable conversation archive, and accept cloud processing and a visible bot.

5. Granola — meeting notes as a knowledge base

Overview. Granola is a bot-free AI notepad: the desktop app captures your mic and system audio locally, streams it to Deepgram/AssemblyAI for transcription, and its AI (OpenAI/Anthropic) fleshes out your rough jottings into polished notes. You jot, it writes. Like Otter, it's a second brain for meetings only, but the capture model is the opposite: no bot, no visible participant, works with anything playing through your speakers.

What we like. Note quality is the best-reviewed in the category; Nat Friedman called it "the best meeting notes of anything I've tried." The human-in-the-loop model (your rough notes shape the AI's output) gives you control that fully automatic summaries don't. No bot means no awkward "OtterPilot has joined" moment, and it covers in-person meetings via the mobile app. The company is SOC 2 Type 2 certified and publishes security post-mortems.

Drawbacks. Audio is never stored, so there's no playback and no way to verify the transcript against the recording. If the notes are wrong, you can't check. Speaker identification is weak: desktop defaults to "Me" and "Them," with named speakers only via a Google Meet extension or Zoom on macOS. Granola's AI runs in its cloud, and Granola trains on anonymized data by default on consumer tiers (opt-out in Settings). Its official pages describe chat within and across meetings but do not publish whether answers link to precise transcript timestamps. The pricing page says Basic has limited meeting history without publishing the exact cap.

Pricing (checked 2026-08-04, granola.ai/pricing). Basic free (AI meeting notes, "limited meeting history," cross-meeting chat); the page does not publish the exact history cap. Business $14/user/mo (unlimited notes and history, integrations, MCP, API). Enterprise $35/user/mo (SSO, admin controls, model training off for the team). No annual discount shown.

Best for. People who want excellent notes from their conversations with zero bot awkwardness, and who treat meetings, rather than documents or screen work, as their knowledge base.

Honorable mentions

Obsidian keeps plain Markdown files locally and has no first-party AI; community plugins add local or provider-backed AI.

Reflect syncs notes with XChaCha20-Poly1305 end-to-end encryption, while its optional AI features send selected text or audio to cloud providers.

Fireflies.ai is a cloud meeting bot with transcription in 100+ languages and API access on every tier.

How to choose

Ask two questions.

Where does your knowledge live? If it's mostly spoken in meetings, Otter or Granola will cover you; pick Otter for archive depth and integrations, Granola for note quality and no bot. If your knowledge is scattered across documents, code, browsing, messages, and meetings, a meeting-only tool structurally can't be your second brain; you need day-wide capture, which on this list means Everpaper or ScreenPipe.

Who do you want reading it? Plain cloud the vendor can read (Mem, Otter), cloud with training opt-outs (Granola), or on-device where nothing leaves the machine unless you say so (Everpaper, ScreenPipe). For a knowledge base that will eventually contain your candid work life, this decision matters more than any feature.

Then the tiebreaker: will you keep feeding it? Manual capture (Mem, Obsidian) works beautifully for people with an iron note-taking habit. Everyone else should weight automatic capture heavily. A second brain with gaps is worse than none, because you'll trust it anyway.

FAQ

What is an AI second brain?

A second brain is a personal knowledge base that an AI can search, so you can ask "what do I know about X" instead of remembering where you put it. AI adds semantic retrieval and chat over the store. The catch is the input side: traditional second brains only contain what you manually saved. Newer tools such as Everpaper and ScreenPipe capture work automatically, which broadens the record and raises the privacy stakes.

Do second brain apps work offline?

Some do. Everpaper's capture, OCR, search, knowledge graph, beliefs, and recall cards all work offline. Its generative AI features wait for a provider, and a local one (Ollama, Apple Foundation Models) keeps even those on your Mac. ScreenPipe works fully offline for capture, transcription, and search. Mem, Otter, and Granola require connectivity for their core AI features.

Is my data safe in a cloud second brain?

Cloud second brains have different disclosure and access models. Mem encrypts at rest and in transit but is explicitly not end-to-end encrypted, so its servers can read your notes to power the AI. Otter's privacy policy reserves the right to train on de-identified recordings and transcripts. Granola trains on anonymized data by default on consumer tiers with an opt-out. If your second brain will hold sensitive work, on-device processing avoids vendor-readable storage.

Can a meeting notetaker be my whole second brain?

Only if meetings are your whole knowledge life. Otter and Granola build real, searchable memory of your conversations, and for sales calls, interviews, or standups that's valuable. They see nothing of the documents you read, the code you wrote, or the research you did between calls. For most knowledge workers that's most of the picture.

What's the difference between Mem and Everpaper?

Capture and location. Mem is manual capture into a cloud service: you write notes, its servers read them to power the AI, and the result is excellent chat over what you remembered to save. Everpaper captures your working day automatically on your Mac, builds the knowledge graph locally, verifies every AI answer against the captured record, and sends nothing anywhere unless you opt into a cloud provider. Mem is a great notebook; Everpaper aims to be the memory you'd have if you never forgot.

Do I need Apple Silicon for Everpaper?

Yes. macOS 14.2 or later on an Apple Silicon Mac; the on-device speech models run on the Neural Engine, and Intel Macs aren't supported. There's no mobile or web version; the design bet is that your working memory lives where you work.
