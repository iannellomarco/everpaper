Best Mac Productivity Apps 2026: 5 Category Winners

The best Mac productivity apps 2026 across memory, notes, meetings, capture, and journaling, with honest hardware requirements and dated pricing.

By Marco Iannello · Updated August 4, 2026

Most "best Mac apps" lists rank forty tools you'll never install. This one picks five, one winner per category, so you end up with a small stack that covers notes, meetings, memory, capture, and journaling without overlap.

One disclosure up front: Everpaper is our product. We make it, we rank it first in the memory category, and we think it earns that spot. But we'll be specific about where it falls short: it needs an Apple Silicon Mac on macOS 14.2 or later, it's in private beta, and it has no mobile or web app. Where another tool wins, we say so.

How we evaluated

We picked one winner per category instead of ranking five apps that do the same thing. The criteria:

  • Does it own its category? A notes app and a journal don't compete. Each pick had to be the tool we'd actually install for that job on a fresh Mac.
  • Where does your data go? We read each vendor's security or privacy page and noted who processes what: on-device, encrypted cloud, or plain cloud.
  • Hardware honesty. Everpaper requires Apple Silicon; ScreenPipe documents Intel support. We state what each vendor's official pages publish.
  • Real pricing, dated. Every published price comes from the vendor's own pricing page, checked 2026-08-04.

1. Everpaper: best for memory and second brain

Everpaper is a private, on-device daybook for macOS: a day journal, your meetings, and a second brain, all kept on your machine. It's our product, so weigh that, but here's the honest version.

Overview. Three pillars. Day Journal takes periodic screenshots of the front window (default every 2 minutes), OCRs them on-device, and writes an AI day narrative you can chat with at the end of the day. The Minutes records meetings (it recognizes Zoom, Google Meet, Teams, Webex, Around, and Whereby links) and transcribes on-device with speaker labels; start one with ⇧⌘M. Second Brain builds a cross-day knowledge graph of people, files, sites, projects, and topics, plus one-line "beliefs" with confidence markers, computed locally. Recall runs through The Index (⌘K) and Ask the Book (⇧⌘A), which answers with a sources list where each source is the exact captured frame; click it and you see the original screen.

What we like. The privacy model is the point. Capture, OCR, transcription (Parakeet parakeet-tdt-0.6b-v3 on the Neural Engine), storage, the knowledge graph, beliefs, and search all run locally with no account. "No provider, no traffic." AI features wait until you configure one, and local providers (Ollama, LM Studio, oMLX, Apple Foundation Models) keep everything on the Mac. A redaction pass scrubs API keys, tokens, and card numbers at capture time and again before any outbound AI call. Export is real: the whole journal exports as a linked Obsidian vault in plain Markdown, plus GraphML and Mermaid graphs.

Drawbacks. macOS 14.2 or later, Apple Silicon required, so Intel Macs are unsupported, full stop. No iOS, Windows, Linux, Android, or web version. It's in private beta, so you can't just download it today. The Second Brain has a readiness gate (5 days, 300 frames, 60 distinct things, 3 recurring threads) before it shows much. And without an AI provider, you get capture, search, and deterministic recall cards; the narrative, chat, and reflection features stay off.

Pricing. In private beta with limited spots, with a founding-license option for early supporters, not a monthly subscription.

Best for. Mac users who want Rewind-style total recall without sending their screen to anyone's cloud, and who live on an Apple Silicon machine. See how the Day Journal works and the privacy model.

2. Obsidian: best notes and PKM

Overview. Obsidian is a local-first personal knowledge manager: plain-text Markdown "vaults" with wiki-style links between notes, a graph view, and 2,000+ community plugins. Capture is manual: you type, or clip via the first-party Web Clipper. There's no meeting bot, no screen capture, no first-party AI.

What we like. True data ownership. Notes are Markdown files on your disk, readable by any tool forever, and the company states it collects no telemetry and never sells user data (pricing page, checked 2026-08-04). Since 2025-02-20 the app is free for personal and commercial use, with no account and no ads. Its 2,000-plus community plugins include Smart Connections for local-embedding semantic search and Copilot for Obsidian for chat-with-vault against whichever LLM you configure, including local models via Ollama.

Drawbacks. The learning curve is real. Markdown, vaults, and plugin configuration overwhelm newcomers, a recurring theme in r/ObsidianMD threads. The mobile app gets consistent complaints about slow startup and lag on large vaults. Whether note text leaves your device depends entirely on which plugin and provider you configure: Smart Connections' default embeddings are local, but Copilot-style chat typically sends vault context to a cloud LLM. And there's no automatic capture. Obsidian remembers what you write down, not what you did. (Everpaper's Obsidian vault export exists precisely so the two can pair.)

Pricing (checked 2026-08-04). App: $0, including commercial use. Obsidian Sync: Standard $4/user/mo billed annually ($5 monthly; 1 vault, 1 GB) or Plus $8/user/mo billed annually ($10 monthly; 10 vaults, 10 GB). Publish: $8/site/mo billed annually. Catalyst: $25 one-time. Source: obsidian.md/pricing.

Best for. Writers, researchers, and developers who want notes they own outright. Obsidian is available on macOS, Windows, Linux, iOS, and Android.

3. Granola: best meeting notes

Overview. Granola is an AI meeting notepad that captures microphone and system audio locally, with no bot joining your call, and turns your rough typed notes plus the transcript into polished meeting notes. It works with anything that plays through system audio: Zoom, Meet, Teams, or an in-person conversation via the mobile app.

What we like. Note quality is Granola's calling card. Nat Friedman called it "the best meeting notes of anything I've tried," and long-term users report it replaced their whole note-taking workflow. The human-in-the-loop design matters: you jot rough notes during the call, and the AI fleshes them out instead of hallucinating a summary from scratch. No-bot capture means no awkward "Granola Notetaker has joined" announcement. It's also genuinely cross-platform: macOS 13+ (best on 14.2+), Windows, iOS, and Android. The web app at notes.granola.ai is view/edit only.

Drawbacks. The audio and transcript leave your Mac. Granola's security page states it uses Deepgram and AssemblyAI for transcription and OpenAI and Anthropic for summaries, stores notes in US-hosted AWS, and trains its own models on anonymized user data by default on consumer tiers (opt-out in Settings; off by default on Enterprise). There's no recording playback, since audio is never stored, so you can't verify the transcript against what was said. Speaker attribution defaults to "Me"/"Them"; named speaker tags exist only for Google Meet and Zoom. Sign-in requires a Google or Microsoft account.

Pricing (checked 2026-08-04, granola.ai/pricing). Basic: $0 with limited meeting history. Business: $14/user/month (unlimited history, integrations, API, MCP). Enterprise: $35/user/month (SSO, admin controls, training off for the team).

Best for. Managers and consultants who want the best-looking notes in the category and are comfortable with cloud processing. If you want the same job done on-device, that's the gap Everpaper's Minutes was built for, at the cost of Apple Silicon-only support and no mobile app.

4. ScreenPipe: best open capture pipeline

Overview. ScreenPipe is a 24/7 local recorder (screen at 1 FPS plus microphone/system audio) with OCR, local Whisper transcription, a SQLite database, a local HTTP API at localhost:3030, and "pipes": scheduled AI agents that act on your captured context (meeting ends → summary → Obsidian note). It's the developer's take on the Rewind idea.

What we like. Genuinely local-first: data lives in ~/.screenpipe/ on your machine, capture and search work fully offline, and a default-on PII-removal flag redacts emails, phone, and card numbers. The local API plus an MCP server for Claude Desktop, Cursor, and ChatGPT make it the most hackable tool on this list. It's written in Rust, runs on macOS (Apple Silicon and Intel; Apple Silicon gets better battery life and faster local Whisper), Windows 10/11, and Linux CLI, and it's Y Combinator backed.

Drawbacks. The license moved from MIT to the "Screenpipe Commercial License" on 2026-06-09 (source-available, free for personal use, paid for commercial use of the source), which read as a rug-pull to parts of the community. The business model has confused people since 2024 ("Is anyone else confused about the business model of Screenpipe?" was an Ask HN). Its own troubleshooting docs tell users to lower capture FPS and use whisper-tiny on older machines. CPU and battery are real concerns. And it's a toolkit, not a finished product: you get raw capture plus building blocks, and the polish of day narratives, readiness-gated second brains, and chat-with-your-day isn't there out of the box. The official documentation reviewed does not state whether speaker diarization is supported.

Pricing (checked 2026-08-04). Free CLI (npx -y screenpipe@latest record) and self-built desktop app for personal use. The paid desktop app is offered with signed builds, auto-updates, and support through subscription or lifetime licensing; its official crawlable pages do not publish the current price.

Best for. Developers and tinkerers who want a local capture layer to build their own agents on, and who'd rather assemble a pipeline than buy a polished daybook.

5. Day One: best journaling

Overview. Day One is the fifteen-year incumbent of digital journaling: manual entries with photos, video, audio (transcribed on Silver+), drawings, scans, templates, prompts, streaks, On This Day, and printed hardcover books of your journal. Automattic-owned since 2021.

What we like. Platform coverage no one else on this list matches: Mac, iPhone, iPad, Apple Watch, Android, Windows, and a web app. End-to-end encryption (AES-GCM-256) is default-on for journals created after v4.2 on iOS/Mac, with the key escrowed to your own iCloud or Google Drive. Day One states it can't read your encrypted entries and will never sell journal data. The April 2026 Gold tier added Daily Chat guided reflection, AI summaries, Go Deeper prompts, and image generation. The official pages reviewed did not state where Day One's own AI features process journal content.

Drawbacks. It's a journal, not a memory system. No screen capture, no meetings, no passive logging; if you didn't type it, Day One doesn't know it happened. Sync runs through Day One's own cloud, not iCloud, which some long-time users resent. Sync reliability complaints and a standing "Known Issues" page persist on r/dayoneapp.

Pricing (checked 2026-08-04, dayoneapp.com/plans). Basic: $0 (1 photo per entry, 1 device). Silver: $49.99/year (unlimited media, multi-device sync, audio transcription). Gold: $74.99/year (adds the AI features, with daily usage limits).

Best for. Anyone building a deliberate journaling habit across Apple and non-Apple devices, and anyone who wants a printed book of their year.

How to choose

Ask four questions:

  1. What hardware do you have? Everpaper requires Apple Silicon. ScreenPipe documents Intel support; the official pages reviewed for Obsidian, Granola, and Day One do not state an Apple Silicon-only requirement.
  2. Where may your data go? Fully local only → Everpaper, Obsidian (with local plugins), ScreenPipe. Day One's synced journals are E2EE, while the official pages reviewed did not state where its own AI features process journal content. Granola sends audio and text to named cloud providers.
  3. Capture or compose? Obsidian and Day One record what you choose to write. Everpaper and ScreenPipe record what you did. Granola records what was said in meetings. Buy for the one that matches how you actually work.
  4. Build or buy? ScreenPipe is a construction kit. Everything else here is a finished product.

FAQ

What productivity apps should every Mac have?

Start with a notes app you own (Obsidian) and something that remembers for you (Everpaper on Apple Silicon, or ScreenPipe if you want to build your own). Add Granola if your calendar is wall-to-wall calls, and Day One if you keep a journal. That's the whole stack: five apps with distinct jobs.

What's the best free Mac productivity app in 2026?

Obsidian. The app is free for personal and commercial use with no account, no ads, and no tracking (obsidian.md/pricing, checked 2026-08-04). Granola's Basic tier and Day One's Basic tier are also genuinely usable at $0, with caps.

Which of these apps need Apple Silicon?

Only Everpaper requires Apple Silicon (and macOS 14.2+); its speech model runs on the Neural Engine. ScreenPipe ships a universal build that runs on Intel too, with better battery life on Apple Silicon. The official pages reviewed for Obsidian, Granola, and Day One do not state an Apple Silicon-only requirement.

Are on-device AI apps actually more private?

The capture and storage layers are what matter most. Everpaper keeps screenshots, transcripts, and its knowledge graph on your Mac with no account, and its AI features stay off until you configure a provider. ScreenPipe stores everything in a local SQLite database and works offline. With Granola, audio goes to Deepgram/AssemblyAI and text to OpenAI/Anthropic. SOC 2 certified, yes, but it is cloud processing. "On-device" is a verifiable claim; read the vendor's security page before trusting the label.

Can I use Everpaper and Obsidian together?

Yes, and it's a designed workflow. Everpaper exports your whole journal as a linked Markdown Obsidian vault: Days, People, Links, Files, Topics folders plus a graph report. Everpaper captures; Obsidian is where you think in long form.

What happened to Rewind?

Rewind is gone. The company rebranded to Limitless, got acquired by Meta (announced December 2025), and the Rewind Mac app shut down on 2025-12-19. Limitless itself is winding down under Meta. If you came here from an old "Rewind alternatives" search, the on-device successors are Everpaper (polished, Apple Silicon only, private beta) and ScreenPipe (source-available, cross-platform, DIY).
