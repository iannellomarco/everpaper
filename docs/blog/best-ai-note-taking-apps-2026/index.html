Best AI Note Taking Apps 2026: 6 Ranked & Compared

The best AI note taking apps in 2026, ranked and compared, from apps where you write every word to a private on-device Mac daybook that writes notes for you.

By Marco Iannello · Updated August 4, 2026

Most "best AI note taking apps 2026" lists pretend the category is one thing. It isn't. The apps on this list disagree about something basic: who writes the notes.

The first split is whether you write. Obsidian and Reflect assist text you compose, while Mem organizes material you save. Otter and Granola produce meeting notes from captured audio. Everpaper takes a different route: the Mac watches the workday and writes the notes itself. That distinction matters more than any feature checklist, so this ranking is organized around it.

How we evaluated

We scored each app on five things: who does the writing, where the data goes, what the AI actually does versus what the marketing says, pricing honesty, and platform coverage. One disclosure, up front: Everpaper is our product. It's ranked first here because for the "I don't want to write notes at all" reader it genuinely is the only option in its camp. We've tried to be as blunt about its limits as we are about everyone else's.

1. Everpaper: the notes you never had to write

Overview. Everpaper is a private, on-device daybook for macOS. It doesn't wait for you to type anything. The Day Journal takes periodic screenshots of your front window (every 2 minutes by default; 30 seconds to 10 minutes configurable), runs OCR on-device, and an AI writes a narrative of your day every 30 minutes. The Minutes records meetings with ⇧⌘M, transcribes on-device with the Parakeet parakeet-tdt-0.6b-v3 model on the Neural Engine, and extracts action items into Today's Focus automatically when the meeting ends. The result: a day narrative, meeting minutes, and a to-do list you never typed.

What we like. No other app on this list produces notes with zero input. The Index (⌘K) searches locally without an AI provider, and deterministic recall cards return phone numbers, emails, links, and files with time-and-place stamps. Ask the Book (⇧⌘A) writes provider-backed answers from matching days and frames; it can remain offline when configured with a local provider. Capture, OCR, transcription, storage, and the knowledge graph all run locally. Provider choices include Ollama, LM Studio, oMLX, Apple Foundation Models, or a cloud API. A redaction pass scrubs API keys, tokens, and card numbers before outbound calls. Everything exports to a plain-Markdown Obsidian vault, so there's no lock-in.

Drawbacks. macOS 14.2+ only, Apple Silicon only; Intel Macs are out. No iPhone app, no web app, no Windows. Access is limited during the private beta. The Second Brain knowledge graph has a readiness gate (5 days, 300 frames, 60 distinct things, 3 recurring threads) before it says "Ready." And the AI-written features wait until you configure a provider; capture and search work without one, the prose doesn't.

Pricing. In private beta with limited spots, with a founding-license option for early supporters, not a monthly subscription. No public price.

Best for. Mac users who want their notes written for them, the full day, not just meetings, and who'd rather keep the raw material on their own disk than in someone else's cloud. See how the Day Journal works and how meeting minutes work.

2. Mem: you capture, AI organizes

Overview. Mem is the strongest pure "AI-native notes" app: you toss text, PDFs, and email into it, and its Mem Agent handles organization and retrieval. The pitch is a self-organizing workspace: skip folders, skip tags, ask the agent later.

What we like. Capture is genuinely low-friction, and reviewers consistently praise the "dump it in, find it later" loop. AI chat across your whole knowledge base is among the best in any note app. Pricing is simple: flat monthly, no per-seat math. There's a one-click Markdown export of everything, and Mem's FAQ commits to never selling data or mining it for ads.

Drawbacks. Mem's own pricing FAQ states your data "is encrypted at rest with AES-256 and in transit with TLS, but it is not e2e encrypted... because our core focus is to provide you with intelligence on top of your Mem." Mem's servers can read your notes. It's cloud-only with no local-vault mode, and sign-in requires a Google or Google Workspace account. The free tier (25 notes and 25 agent messages a month) is a trial in practice. Reddit's r/PKMS has recurring threads about slow development and a weaker editor than Notion or Obsidian.

Pricing (checked 2026-08-04). Free $0 (25 notes/mo, 25 Mem Agent messages, 25 PDF pages). Pro $12/mo monthly, no annual discount (unlimited notes, 250 agent messages, unlimited PDF chat, Meeting Briefings). Proactive $99/mo after a 7-day trial (agent briefs you before meetings and tracks open loops).

Best for. People who already live in Google accounts, don't mind cloud processing, and want the best AI chat over notes they've written themselves.

3. Reflect: you write, encrypted, with an AI palette

Overview. Reflect is a networked note-taking app built around a daily note, backlinks, and end-to-end encrypted sync. Notes are encrypted client-side with XChaCha20-Poly1305 before they leave your device, so Reflect's servers store blobs they can't read, and the cryptography passed an independent Doyensec audit. Its native clients are for macOS, iPhone, and iPad (TestFlight); it also offers a web app and browser clippers.

What we like. This is real E2EE, verified by an outside auditor, from a bootstrapped indie team that's been shipping since 2021. The AI palette (⌘J) transforms highlighted text with GPT-4o or Claude, and voice memos get transcribed via Whisper. Calendar integration links meeting notes to events, and there's even an MCP server in beta so coding agents can read your notes.

Drawbacks. The E2EE boundary stops where AI starts: highlighted text and voice audio go to OpenAI, Anthropic, or Google, and Reflect's privacy policy admits those providers "may use prompts we send them as training data." No Windows or Android app is offered. There is no free tier, only a 14-day trial. Lose your password and your notes are gone permanently; that's the price of real encryption. Paid accounts cap AI at 100k tokens/day unless you bring your own API key.

Pricing (checked 2026-08-04). One plan: $10/mo billed annually ($120/year), everything included. 14-day free trial. No monthly billing shown on the current site.

Best for. Writers and thinkers on Apple hardware who want encrypted sync and are fine sending selected snippets to cloud AI on demand.

4. Granola: cloud AI meeting notes, no bot

Overview. Granola covers the meeting slice of note-taking. The desktop app captures your microphone and system audio locally (no bot ever joins the call) and you jot rough notes while it transcribes. Afterward, AI fleshes your jottings into structured notes. It works with any meeting platform on Mac or Windows, plus iOS and Android for in-person conversations.

What we like. Note quality is widely praised; Nat Friedman called it the best meeting notes of anything he'd tried. The human-in-the-loop model (you sketch, AI fills in) gives you more control than a fully automated summary. There's no awkward "OtterPilot has joined" moment. Audio is never stored, only transcript and notes, and the company holds SOC 2 Type 2.

Drawbacks. "On your computer" refers to capture only. Audio streams to Deepgram or AssemblyAI for transcription and text goes to OpenAI or Anthropic for notes, all in Granola's AWS cloud, and Granola trains its own models on anonymized data by default on consumer tiers (opt-out in Settings). Speaker attribution is weak: desktop transcripts default to "Me" and "Them" labels. Named speakers are limited to Google Meet through a browser extension and Zoom on macOS. No recording playback means you can't verify the transcript against audio. Sign-in requires a Google or Microsoft account.

Pricing (checked 2026-08-04). Basic is free with "limited meeting history"; the official pricing page does not publish the exact history cap. Business is $14/user/mo (unlimited history, integrations, API). Enterprise is $35/user/mo (SSO, admin controls, training off by default).

Best for. People who want excellent meeting notes without a bot in the call and are comfortable with cloud processing.

5. Otter.ai: the bot that writes your meeting notes

Overview. Otter is the incumbent meeting bot. OtterPilot joins your Zoom, Google Meet, or Teams calls as a visible participant, transcribes live, identifies speakers, captures slides, and emails a summary. It also records in-app on desktop and mobile, imports audio files, and offers AI chat across your whole meeting archive.

What we like. Mature live transcription with real-time editing and comments. The broadest platform coverage here: bot, web, Mac, Windows, iOS, Android, Chrome extension. Cross-meeting AI chat over an entire archive is genuinely useful, and the Salesforce/HubSpot/Zapier integrations fit sales workflows.

Drawbacks. A bot in the call is a consent problem, and it's now a legal one: Otter faces a consolidated federal class action (In re Otter.AI Privacy Litigation, N.D. Cal.) alleging recording without all-party consent, with a motion-to-dismiss hearing held May 20, 2026. Its privacy policy (effective June 16, 2026) reserves the right to train "proprietary AI technology on de-identified audio recordings and on transcriptions." Accuracy reviews are mixed, and the free tier's 300 monthly minutes with a 30-minute per-conversation cap runs out fast.

Pricing (checked 2026-08-04). Basic free (300 min/mo, 30 min/conversation, 3 lifetime file imports). Pro $16.99/user/mo monthly or $8.49/user/mo billed annually (1,200 min/mo, 90-min meetings). Business $30/user/mo monthly or $24/user/mo billed annually (unlimited meetings, 4-hour cap).

Best for. Teams that want a searchable archive of every call and don't mind a visible bot or cloud training terms. For a broader set of options, see our best AI meeting assistants ranking.

6. Obsidian: you write everything, plugins add AI

Overview. Obsidian is local-first Markdown note-taking: plain-text files in a folder you own, readable by any tool forever. The core app has no AI at all. AI arrives through community plugins — Smart Connections for local-embedding semantic search, Copilot for Obsidian for chat-with-vault against a provider you configure yourself.

What we like. Total data ownership. Files stay on your disk, there's no telemetry, no account, and since February 2025 the app is free for commercial use too. Smart Connections runs a local embedding model with zero setup and no API key. With 2,000+ community plugins, you can build almost any workflow, and if you outgrow a plugin your notes are still just Markdown.

Drawbacks. You write every word. Nothing captures your day, records a meeting, or summarizes anything unless you set it up and drive it. The learning curve is real: Markdown, vault structure, and plugin configuration lose a lot of newcomers. Mobile is a common complaint (slow startup, clunky capture). And whether your notes touch a cloud LLM depends entirely on which plugin you install and how you configure it; the privacy you get is the privacy you build.

Pricing (checked 2026-08-04). Core app free, including commercial use. Sync Standard $4/mo annual (1 vault, 1 GB), Sync Plus $8/mo annual (10 vaults, 10 GB), Publish $8/site/mo annual, Catalyst $25 one-time. Commercial license $50/user/year, now optional.

Best for. Tinkerers who want plain-text files, total control, and are happy wiring up their own AI. We compared it head-to-head with our approach in Obsidian vs Everpaper.

How to choose

Start with the honest question: who do you want writing your notes?

If you want AI to edit text you write, Reflect offers encrypted sync on Apple devices, while Obsidian keeps local files under your control. Mem provides cloud AI over saved material.

For meeting-only notes, Granola avoids a participant bot; Otter provides a visible bot, an archive, and CRM integrations. For a machine-written record across the day, Everpaper is the option here for an Apple Silicon Mac.

Then apply the filters. Windows or Android requirements rule out Reflect and Everpaper. On-device-only rules rule out Mem, Granola, and Otter. Compare subscription costs and published free-tier limits over the period you expect to keep the app.

FAQ

What is the best free AI note taking app? Obsidian, and it isn't close: the full app is free even for commercial use, with local files and no account, and the Smart Connections plugin adds local AI search at no cost. For meeting notes specifically, Otter's free tier (300 minutes/month) and Granola's Basic plan both work, but both process your audio in the cloud. Mem's free tier is capped at 25 notes a month, which is a trial in practice.

Can AI take notes for me automatically? Yes, in two flavors. Meeting bots and capture tools like Otter and Granola write notes from call audio with no typing. Everpaper goes further: it watches your workday on a Mac (screenshots every 2 minutes, on-device OCR, an AI-written day narrative every 30 minutes) plus automatic meeting minutes with action items, all without you writing a word. What it can't do is know what you were thinking; a one-tap Mark Moment note covers that.

Are AI note taking apps private? It depends where the processing happens. Obsidian (local files) and Everpaper (on-device capture, transcription, and search) keep data on your machine. Reflect encrypts notes end-to-end but sends highlighted text and voice audio to OpenAI/Anthropic/Google for AI features. Mem is not end-to-end encrypted by its own FAQ. Otter's privacy policy reserves training rights on de-identified recordings, and Granola trains on anonymized data by default.

What's the difference between an AI meeting assistant and a note taking app? Scope. Meeting assistants (Otter, Granola) capture calls and produce minutes. Note taking apps (Mem, Reflect, Obsidian) store and organize what you write. Everpaper sits across both: it records meetings and journals the rest of the day, so meeting notes land next to everything else you did.

Which AI note taking apps work offline? Obsidian works fully offline; it's local Markdown. Everpaper captures, transcribes, and searches offline; its AI-written features pause until you configure a provider, which can itself be local (Ollama, LM Studio, oMLX, Apple Foundation Models). Mem, Granola, and Otter all require connectivity for their core AI features.

Do AI note taking apps train on my data? Otter's policy explicitly reserves training on de-identified audio and transcripts. Granola trains on anonymized data by default on consumer tiers (opt-out available; off on Enterprise). Reflect's policy admits its AI providers may train on prompts. Mem says it never sells data but processes it server-side. Obsidian collects no data; Everpaper processes on-device and redacts secrets before any optional cloud call.
